A quieter week on the architecture side, with two papers digging into exactly what attention heads are actually doing — one as a diagnostic tool, one as a theory result explaining why real transformers look nothing like the worst-case instances — plus a fix for a pair of quietly-wrong gradient computations that have likely been in production loss functions for years.

SVD-Attention: Efficiently Analyzing Attention Blocks in Transformers

ML/AI
arXiv:2610.34971 · submitted Oct 26, 2026

Rather than proposing a new compression trick, this paper builds a diagnostic: it decomposes each attention head's query-key interaction and scores every singular mode by how much it actually matters on real data, flagging which heads are using their full rank budget and which are coasting on redundant capacity. The payoff is precision — truncating the layers it flags costs at most 1.75 points of F1, while truncating the same number of layers elsewhere in the same model can cost up to 50 points — a much sharper tool for deciding where a model can safely be cut than rank or magnitude heuristics alone.

Attention under Bounded Key-Query Similarity: Space Complexity and Transformer Anisotropy

ML/AI
arXiv:2610.35020 · MIT/EPFL · submitted Oct 26, 2026

Recent tight bounds on how much memory attention approximation needs in the worst case are achieved by instances where queries align perfectly with keys — but real transformers are famously anisotropic, with query-key cosine similarities clustered tightly around zero, not spread out to allow such alignment. This paper redoes the space-complexity analysis under that more realistic constraint, giving a nearly space-optimal algorithm with a matching lower bound, and shows its hard instances actually reproduce the anisotropy seen empirically — closing the gap between what worst-case theory predicts and what real KV-cache compression work has been observing in practice.

A Fix for Softmax and Cross-Entropy

ML/AI
arXiv:2610.31642 · Barron, Gnanapragasam, Tschannen · submitted Oct 22, 2026

Identifies two previously under-appreciated floating-point bugs baked into standard softmax and cross-entropy implementations: "softmax collapse," where a dominant logit's gradient silently rounds to zero so gradients no longer sum to zero, and a newly-named "softmax cancellation," where near-identical student and teacher logits still produce spurious non-zero gradients. Both would quietly corrupt distillation and fine-tuning signal in ways that are easy to miss in a loss curve. The authors ship drop-in replacements for log-softmax, softmax, and cross-entropy that fix both — a rare case of a correctness bug this basic, this late in the deep learning stack.

Causal Chain of Thought: Mitigating Attention Leakage via Context Isolation

ML/AI
Dominici, McMillan, Barez, Langheinrich, Gjoreski · preprint, Oct 22, 2026

Chain-of-thought rationales look like they explain a model's answer, but there's no guarantee the written steps are what's actually driving the output rather than post-hoc narration. This paper's fix, CausalCoT, splits a problem into a graph of sub-questions and evaluates each one in a restricted context that can only see its direct causal parents, cutting off attention to irrelevant earlier steps. The reported gain — 12 points on average, up to 21 — in how causally load-bearing each reasoning step actually is, without sacrificing accuracy, is a concrete step toward chain-of-thought you can trust rather than just read.

On the Open Problem in the 2023 Simon Marais Mathematics Competition

Math
arXiv:2610.30658 · Anthony Pisani, Monash University · submitted Oct 22, 2026

The 2023 Simon Marais competition (an undergraduate contest modeled on the Putnam) posed an open question: for which pairs of positive integers (a, b) does there exist a real number r such that both r^a + √n and r^b + √n are rational, for n not a perfect square? This paper answers it completely — exactly the pairs with a = b, or {a, b} = {g, 2g} for odd g — and, per the author, resolves the first open problem from the competition's nine-year history to actually get solved. A Lean formalization of the main theorem is already up on GitHub, sorry-free.

Nonvanishing Implies Abundance

Math
arXiv:2610.35913 · Jihao Liu · submitted Oct 28, 2026

The abundance conjecture is one of the central open problems in the classification theory of algebraic varieties (the minimal model program), and the author's comment on this submission says plainly that he posted an unfinished-but-solid draft early specifically because of OpenAI's announced claim to have proved it. The paper proves the general reduction — that the (weaker, more tractable) nonvanishing conjecture implies abundance — extending the author's own 2025 result, which only covered a restricted numerical-dimension case. A rare visible instance of a human mathematician's publication schedule being reshaped in real time by a disputed AI claim.

The Critical 2D KPZ Process

Math
arXiv:2610.31614 · Berger, Caravenna, Zygouras, et al. (11 authors) · submitted Oct 22, 2026

The two-dimensional KPZ equation sits at a notoriously hard critical dimension where the usual singular-SPDE machinery stops applying cleanly. Building on the recent construction of the "critical 2D stochastic heat flow," this large collaboration constructs the corresponding critical KPZ process itself — a genuinely non-Gaussian, distribution-valued object obtained as the critical-point limit of 2D directed polymer free energies. It's the kind of foundational-object paper that a whole sub-literature on 2D disordered systems will likely build on for years.

How Voting Is Like a Klein Bottle

Blog
Quanta Magazine, Alex Stone · Oct 26, 2026

Condorcet's 1785 discovery that majority voting can produce logical cycles — where every candidate loses head-to-head to someone else, even though each voter's own ranking is perfectly consistent — turns out to have a precise geometric shape. Graduate student Ori Livson and collaborators show the space of such cyclic, "unrealized" preference patterns is homeomorphic to a Klein bottle, the classic one-sided surface with no distinct inside or outside. A nice example of topology giving a genuinely new way to picture a 240-year-old paradox, rather than just restating it.
