ML/AI Papers

A reasoning-and-efficiency-heavy week: three papers dig into when and how to spend less compute on long chains of thought — targeted compression, causally testing what memory compaction actually does to an agent's behavior, and a sharper look at distillation versus RL — one probes a popular routing trick's fading returns now that base models already reason well on their own, and a sprawling multi-lab survey asks the uncomfortable question of whether mechanistic interpretability is actually making models safer or just satisfying curiosity.

Think Less, Locally: Local Preference Learning for Reasoning Compression

ML/AI
arXiv:2611.04814 · Muyao Yuan et al. (Xi'an Jiaotong University, WeChat AI/Tencent) · submitted Nov 3, 2026

Most attempts to shorten a model's chain-of-thought compress the whole trace uniformly, which risks cutting corners the model actually needed. LoCo instead finds the specific point mid-reasoning where a model has effectively "done enough" thinking, freezes everything before that point, and regenerates a shorter, verified continuation from there — then trains on these paired long/short traces with direct preference optimization. The reported payoff is real: 42–45% shorter math traces and 23–36% shorter code traces with no drop in accuracy, and training only on math still transfers the compression skill to code, suggesting "knowing when you've reasoned enough" is a somewhat general skill rather than a token-counting trick tied to one domain.

Why Pay for Long Reasoning When Rewrite Do Trick? Causal Effects of Reasoning History Compaction on Future Generation and Agent Cost

ML/AI
arXiv:2611.03852 · Anton Hristov · submitted Nov 2, 2026

Instead of just benchmarking context compaction, this paper asks a causal question: if you rewrite an agent's past reasoning into a compact summary before it sees new tool results, does that actually change how the model reasons afterward, not just how much the call costs? The answer is yes — compaction reliably shortens future reasoning and can meaningfully cut real-dollar costs in software-agent pipelines — but the effect is inconsistent across models and compressors, and for some models it visibly hurts task quality. A useful corrective for anyone treating memory compaction as a free lunch in long-horizon agent loops.

Unpacking the Compute Efficiency of Strong-to-Weak On-Policy Distillation Against GRPO

ML/AI
arXiv:2611.08453 · Zeyu Leo Liu, Yang Li, Bo Pang, et al. (Salesforce AI Research) · submitted Nov 5, 2026

On-policy distillation has been pitched lately as a cheap substitute for reinforcement learning, but this paper complicates that story: distilling from an external, stronger teacher model turns out to be less compute-efficient per unit of accuracy than just running GRPO directly. The twist is that distilling a model's own GRPO checkpoint back into itself recovers the same accuracy for far less compute, and the real advantage shows up when merging several separately-trained domain specialists into one generalist via distillation — which beats training a single model jointly across all domains with GRPO from scratch. A good antidote to treating "RL vs. distillation" as a single universal tradeoff.

When Strong Reasoners Join the Pool: Small Union Premiums and Costly Runaway Generations in LLM Routing

ML/AI
arXiv:2611.04098 · Teng-Ruei Chen · submitted ~Nov 3, 2026

Ensemble routing — querying several LLMs and keeping whichever answer looks right — reportedly gave weaker models a solid ~3.5 percentage-point accuracy boost in earlier work. Tested here against today's strong reasoning models, that gain shrinks to under 0.6 points, and a good chunk of the wasted compute turns out to come from a small number of responses that hit the generation length cap and run away — one model in the pool was simply too slow to ever finish. A concrete, measured illustration that once each individual model is already a strong reasoner, routing between them pays off far less than the ensemble intuition suggests.

Understanding AI from the Inside: The State of Mechanistic Interpretability for AI Safety

ML/AI
arXiv:2611.08438 · Fazl Barez, Tung-Yu Wu, Hunar Batra, et al. — ~25 co-authors incl. Yoshua Bengio, Stella Biderman, Mor Geva · submitted ~Nov 5, 2026

A sprawling, multi-lab survey that asks the question a lot of interpretability work quietly sidesteps: does understanding what's happening inside a model's circuits actually make it safer, or is that connection just assumed? It proposes a framework for checking interpretability claims against real behavioral evals, and surveys where circuit-level understanding has (and hasn't) paid off for tasks like knowledge editing, steering, and monitoring. The author list — spanning most of the field's major interpretability labs and academic groups — makes this read like it's aiming to be the reference point for what mechanistic interpretability can and can't currently promise.

Mathematicians Race to Simplify AI-Found Proofs

Math
Quanta Magazine, "Transformation" updates · Konstantin Kakaes · early Nov 2026 (exact date unconfirmed)

After OpenAI released some 377 claimed proofs on October 6 — including one addressing the long-studied question of whether "Landau–Siegel zeros" exist — mathematicians didn't just check the results, they started competing to rewrite them more simply. Bryce Kerr of UNSW Canberra is one of several who produced a cleaner, human-readable re-proof of the Siegel-zero result after the AI got there first, deliberately avoiding reading the AI's own version too closely before writing his own. It's a glimpse of a new division of labor taking shape: AI finds a correct proof fast, and mathematicians now race each other to "digest" it into something the field can actually understand and build on, rather than letting the literature fill up with correct-but-opaque results.

Sharp Moderate Deviations and Uniform Berry–Esseen Bounds for Hotelling's T²-Statistic via Stein's Method

Math
arXiv:2611.08074 · Song-Hao Liu, Qi-Man Shao, Hao Shi, Jing-Yu Xu · submitted Nov 2026 (math.PR / math.ST)

Hotelling's T² statistic is the standard way to generalize a t-test to many dimensions at once, and this paper uses Stein's method — a classical technique for proving how fast a distribution converges to its Gaussian or chi-squared limit — to get sharp, uniform error bounds for it even in high dimensions. That matters well beyond the proof itself: tighter, more honest convergence bounds are exactly what make p-values and confidence regions trustworthy in the high-dimensional data analysis that's now routine in genomics, finance, and large-scale experimentation.

Sequential Certification of Partially Identified Product Targets: Information Limits and Adaptive Sampling

Math
arXiv:2611.07567 · Abdallah Skiredj et al. · submitted Nov 5, 2026 (math.ST)

Plenty of real decisions — clinical trials, A/B tests — have to be made before the data can fully pin down the answer, when the target quantity is only "partially identified" by what's observable. This paper works out the fundamental information-theoretic limits on how fast you can sequentially gather evidence and still certify a conclusion under that kind of built-in ambiguity, and proposes adaptive sampling schemes that approach those limits. Useful groundwork for anyone trying to speed up sequential testing without quietly overclaiming what the data actually supports.

A Note on the Cut-Locus-Corrected Fréchet Mean Central Limit Theorem

Math
arXiv:2611.07956 · Susovan Pal · submitted Nov 2026 (math.ST / math.PR)

A Fréchet mean extends the ordinary idea of an "average" to curved spaces — shape spaces, manifolds — where you can't just add points together. The usual central limit theorem for these means breaks down near a manifold's cut locus, the set of points where the shortest path between two points stops being unique, and this note works out the correction needed to make the statistics behave there. A small, technical fix, but exactly the kind of foundational patch that statistics on non-Euclidean data — directional data, shape analysis — depends on to stay rigorous.

AI for Scientific Discovery: How Researchers Use Claude

Lab
Anthropic, Economics · Nov 5, 2026

Anthropic combed through roughly 207,000 real Claude and Claude Code research sessions from August–September 2026 to see how scientists actually use AI day to day, rather than how they say they use it. Usage skews heavily toward computer science, math, and statistics — and within that, toward data analysis and writing up results — while idea generation and data collection, the two activities scientists themselves rate as most valuable, get comparatively little AI help. The report estimates current AI could roughly double output in some computational fields (with up to ~15x in a fully-automated hypothetical), versus a much more modest 3–4x ceiling in lab sciences that stay bottlenecked by physical experimentation.

Teaching AI to Build Scientific Models

Lab
Anthropic, Science (guest post) · Nov 6, 2026

Argues AI's real opportunity in science isn't just fitting curves to data but helping build mechanistic models — of neurons, of whole brains — while flagging a real danger: a model that confidently returns one "best-fit" explanation can hide other equally valid ones. Its proposed fix is simulation-based inference: run a candidate model many times, train a neural network to invert simulated outputs back to plausible parameter ranges, and have the AI tool report a spread of plausible models plus which features the data can and can't actually constrain — instead of a single falsely-confident answer.

The Components of Agent Memory

Lab
Anthropic, Engineering · Nov 6, 2026

Draws an analogy between how human memory consolidates during sleep and how AI agents might organize their own memory, introducing "dreaming" — an offline process where Claude reviews its own past session logs, corrects mistaken notes, and builds structured wikis rather than hoarding raw transcripts. The headline result: letting Claude decide for itself what's worth remembering beat fixed rules like "only log failures," and on a long document-QA task, adding a dreamed wiki pushed accuracy from 13% to 48%, with wiki-plus-search reaching 61%. A concrete, measured case for curated memory over simply remembering everything.

Working with circuit-board maker Diode Computers, Anthropic tested whether Claude could carry electronics design end to end — circuit layout, enclosure, firmware, parts list — and came away with three working prototypes (a speakerphone, a voice-assistant hub, a handheld virtual pet) that human engineers built without needing to change the circuits. From Full to Hybrid Attention in LLMs

Blog
Sebastian Raschka · Nov 10, 2026

Raschka argues the field has quietly but decisively moved from full quadratic self-attention toward hybrid designs that mix in a few full or gated-attention layers among many cheap linear-attention or state-space layers — pointing to Qwen3-Next's repeating pattern of three Gated DeltaNet blocks followed by one Gated Attention block, now carried forward into Qwen3.5. He's careful to note the KV-cache savings from this are partial, not total, since the remaining attention layers still need to cache past tokens. A clear, numbers-grounded snapshot of where production attention architecture actually stands heading into 2027, rather than where the papers say it's headed.
