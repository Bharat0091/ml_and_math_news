Energy-Based Reasoners: Your Recurrent Reasoner Should Be an Energy-Based Model

ML/AI
arXiv:2611.08977 · William Xingxu Chen, Alexi Gladstone, Zeyu Michael Li, Heng Ji, Yilun Du · submitted Nov 6, 2026; widely discussed on Hacker News this week

Rather than just stacking more recurrent layers for "thinking," this paper has a reasoning model learn an energy function over its own latent reasoning states and treats each update as a step of gradient descent on that energy — plus an exploration phase during training meant to find multiple good reasoning paths instead of collapsing onto the first one found. The payoff is compute efficiency rather than a raw accuracy jump: the authors report matching or beating prior recurrent reasoners while using roughly 345x less inference compute, reaching 99.9% accuracy on Sudoku-Extreme. It resurfaced in community discussion this week as one of the more concrete "cheap System-2 reasoning" results going around, which is why it's included here a few days outside its original post date.

SciWorldBench: Do Video World Models Really Understand Scientific Experiments?

ML/AI
arXiv:2611.20899 · submitted ~Nov 17, 2026

Joins a small but growing family of benchmarks (SciVideoBench, VideoScience-Bench, ExpVid) built around one pointed question: when a video or world model watches a scientific experiment, is it tracking the actual physics, or just reproducing the visual style of similar-looking footage? Earlier entries in this family have consistently found that even strong video generators get the look of an experiment right while getting its real-world outcome wrong, and SciWorldBench pushes that same scrutiny onto world models being used for planning and prediction, not just video generation. We weren't able to confirm the paper's specific reported numbers independently this week — worth checking the abstract directly before citing figures.

A cluster of papers asking what actually makes agent self-improvement work

ML/AI
arXiv:2611.23219, 2611.23068, 2611.23067, 2611.23138 · all submitted Nov 18–19, 2026

Within about a day of each other, four separate papers landed on arXiv all circling the same open question from different angles. "Do LLM Agents Still Need to Be Told How to Self-Improve?" asks whether explicit self-improvement instructions are still necessary now that models are better at it by default. "The Resolution Hypothesis" argues the real bottleneck in agent evolution isn't generating candidate changes but correctly telling which ones are genuine improvements. A third paper looks at how an agent's instilled "values" shape what it chooses to build when left to iterate on itself, and a fourth ("RSI-Prime") frames the whole problem as training models whose job is to train other models. None of these settle the question on its own, but the sheer concentration of submissions on it in a single week is itself a signal of where a meaningful slice of the field's attention currently sits. ML/AI
arXiv:2611.23045 · submitted ~Nov 19, 2026

As LLM-based forecasting tools get pitched as alternatives to prediction markets and expert panels, this benchmark targets a basic infrastructure gap: grading forecasters against live, future events that actually resolve, replayed under controlled conditions, rather than static historical questions a model could simply have memorized during pretraining. That data-leakage problem has quietly undermined a lot of "AI predicts the future" claims, and a reusable, replayable harness for it is overdue.

On Erdős' Last Equation

Math
arXiv:2611.20783 (companion note: arXiv:2611.20799) · Florian Luca, István Pink, Csaba Sándor · submitted Nov 16, 2026 (math.NT)

"Erdős's last equation" asks about increasing integer tuples (x₁ ≤ ... ≤ xₙ) satisfying n(x₁+...+xₙ) = x₁x₂...xₙ; earlier work by two of these same authors showed the largest term xₙ must grow without bound as n increases. This pair of companion papers pins down more precisely how slowly xₙ is allowed to grow and tightens the bounds on the full solution set — a modest but concrete narrowing of a problem whose roots trace back to Erdős's own notebooks and a 2019 American Mathematical Monthly article revisiting it.

A New Lower Bound for the Erdős Box Problem

Math
arXiv:2611.17378 · Dean Matthew Menezes · submitted ~Nov 2026 (math.CO)

The Erdős box problem asks how large a "box-free" set of integer points in a high-dimensional grid can be — a combinatorics question that's seen steady incremental progress via random and algebraic constructions over the past few years (notably a 2020 improvement using random multilinear maps). This paper reportedly pushes the known lower-bound exponent for the four-dimensional case from 15/4 up to 19/5 via explicit constructions of box-free subsets. We flag this one with slightly less confidence than the rest of this digest, since we couldn't independently cross-check the exact bound against a second source this week — worth a quick abstract check before citing the number.

Claude Solves Two Open Problems in the Physics of Black Holes

Lab
Anthropic, Science · Nov 17, 2026

Anthropic says a Claude model completed two previously-stuck black-hole physics problems: finishing a calculation first attempted in 2003 on whether black-hole formation in small "toy universe" models proceeds through a first-order phase transition, and sharpening a modular-bootstrap bound — used to rule out candidate quantum gravity theories in simplified settings — down to a precise value near 9.1266, where only a rough range (roughly 8.5 to 12.3) was previously known. Two unreviewed preprints posted Nov 7 back the claims, and Anthropic says one result was independently reproduced using the separate, publicly available Claude Opus 5.5. Neither has been peer-reviewed, so treat this as a notable but unverified claim rather than a settled result — the pattern of labs publishing AI-assisted "results" ahead of independent review is becoming its own small trend worth watching critically.

AlphaGenome 2: Deciphering How Cells Read DNA to Better Understand Disease

Lab
Google DeepMind, Science · Nov 11, 2026

The successor to AlphaGenome predicts how a given DNA variant affects gene activity in specific cell types and states, rather than averaging across a whole tissue sample — trained on more than 107 million individual-cell profiles across 68 tissues. On the TenK10K immune-cell benchmark it reportedly recovers 30% of likely-causal variants at 90% precision, roughly double the 16% managed by the original AlphaGenome fine-tuned for single-cell data, and in a retrospective inflammatory-bowel-disease case it computationally reproduced a known regulatory variant's effect on a gut-macrophage gene. It's freely available for non-commercial research via API, though DeepMind is explicit it hasn't been validated for clinical or diagnostic use.
